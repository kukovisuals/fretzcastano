Cellular automata, from a blank grid

There's not much to this, really. It's a walk from an empty grid of squares down to a working GLSL automaton — specifically your Day 449 shader: Conway's rule running on a diamond neighbourhood, with a little built-in microscope. I've kept the code out of it until the very end; instead every idea comes with a diagram or a live demo, and every demo on this page is real GLSL running in your browser, so none of it is hand-waving. If you want the deeper stuff — energy, paint, rule bitmasks — that's over in the sponge reference.

1. A world made of squares

Start with nothing: a grid of squares, all switched off. This grid is the entire universe of a cellular automaton, and there's not much to it. No objects, no particles, nothing you can pick up or move around. Just squares, and each square is always in exactly one of two states: on or off. That's the whole setup.

Time here isn't smooth. It happens in ticks, like a clock: tick, tick, tick. Between ticks, nothing whatsoever happens. At each tick, every square decides — all at once, simultaneously — whether it'll be on or off during the next tick. That decision is the whole physics of the place, and it follows one law that every square obeys identically. Different laws give you different universes; we'll get to the famous one in a second.

Before any of that, get a feel for the stage itself. Below is an empty grid. Click squares to switch them on — this is you playing creator, placing the starting points by hand, one click at a time. Then hit step to advance the clock one tick under Conway's law (coming up next), and watch what your arrangement does:

Try a few tiny experiments before reading on: drop a single square on its own and step (it vanishes — loneliness). Put three in a row and step a few times (they blink forever — the simplest little "creature"). Drop a filled 2×2 block (it just sits there — stability). Everything this whole page builds is made out of behaviours like these.

2. The law: three questions

Here's the law the demo above was quietly following. Every tick, every square asks itself exactly three questions:

Question one — how many of my neighbours are on? A neighbour is a square touching me. Count the ones that are on, call the total k. That single number is the only thing a square is ever allowed to use. It can't see far away, it can't remember last week, it can't know what the pattern "is". Just: how crowded is it right here?

Question two — am I currently on?

Question three — so what does the law say? Conway's law, the famous one, goes like this: if you're off and exactly 3 neighbours are on, switch on (a birth needs three parents). If you're on and 2 or 3 neighbours are on, stay on (comfortable). Otherwise, be off (lonely if fewer, crushed if more). That's the whole thing.

The same law as pictures — the centre square (marked *) is the one doing the deciding, and only its own state plus its count matter:

Flip the show counts overlay on in the demo above and the law becomes visible: each square shows its k, green numbers mark squares about to be born, struck-out squares are about to die. Step slowly and audit any square you like — the whole universe is just these three questions asked everywhere at once.

3. Time, and the photograph

One word in the law hides a big requirement: simultaneously. Every square decides at the same instant, based on how the grid looked before the tick. If squares updated one after another, the early deciders would corrupt the counts of the late deciders and the whole thing would break. So the machine needs two grids: the photograph (last tick, frozen, read-only, everyone consults it) and the canvas (next tick, being written). Once every square has written its answer, the canvas becomes the new photograph and a fresh blank canvas gets prepared. Tick.

This is exactly what a GPU shader hands you, almost by accident. A fragment shader is a little program that runs once per pixel, all pixels in parallel — which is precisely "every square decides simultaneously". And on Shadertoy, wiring Buffer A's own output back into its input channel makes Shadertoy keep two copies of the buffer and swap them every frame: while all pixels write frame N, they read frame N−1. That swap is the ping-pong. One pixel = one square, one frame = one tick, ping-pong = photograph and canvas.

Now the question you actually asked: is the ping-pong what makes it move?

No — the ping-pong is what makes time exist; the law is what makes things move. The ping-pong is just the film advancing in the camera: it guarantees there's a sequence of grids at all. What shows up on those grids is entirely the law's doing. And the law can't move anything, because nothing in this universe is movable — remember, there are only squares bolted to their positions. So when something visibly travels across the screen, what's really going on is: squares just ahead of the pattern are counting exactly 3 and switching on, while squares at the back are going lonely and switching off. The pattern is demolished and rebuilt one square over, tick after tick. Think of a stadium wave — nobody runs anywhere, yet the wave circles the stadium. Or a news ticker: fixed bulbs, travelling headline.

4. The neighbourhood is a shape

Question one said "count your neighbours" — but who counts as a neighbour? That's a choice, and it's a much bigger one than it looks. The classic pick is the 8 squares physically touching you (the Moore neighbourhood). But any set of squares at fixed positions relative to you is a legal neighbourhood, and each choice gives you a physically different universe — different creatures, different textures, different speed limits on how fast patterns can travel. Here's a family of shapes, borrowed from the shader this whole series grew out of:

To pin a shape down precisely, give each square an address relative to me: a pair (i, j) meaning "i squares to my right, j squares up" — negatives mean left and down. My own address is (0, 0). Here's the diamond, the shape your shader uses, with every member's address written in:

Now a lovely bit of theory that your Day 449 quietly proves: Conway's law never mentions the shape. It only says "3 to be born, 2–3 to survive" — numbers that make sense for any neighbourhood with 8 members. So you can lift the law off the Moore square and set it down on the diamond, and you get a perfectly valid, brand-new universe: same physics constants, different geometry of space. The two are not the same universe wearing different clothes — the diamond can't see its four touching diagonal corners but can reach out to distance 2, so information flows through it differently. 5. One click of the dice: the starting points

A law isn't enough — the universe needs a beginning. On tick zero there's no photograph to consult, so the very first frame has to be special: instead of asking the three questions, every square rolls a die once. "Random number under some threshold → I start on." That threshold is the density: at 0.5, roughly half of all squares begin on (your Day 449's pick); at 0.15, a sparse scatter.

Two bits of theory hide in this one-time dice roll. First, a shader has no dice — it's a calculator, always deterministic — so randomness gets faked with a hash: a deliberately chaotic bit of arithmetic (sines of big numbers, ugly constants) that turns each square's position into a value that merely looks random. Same position, same "random" number, which is fine — we only need the pattern to be structureless, not secret. Second, the starting density matters less than you'd think and more than you'd hope: Conway-family laws burn any dense soup down to sparse debris and small stable creatures pretty quickly — the universe forgets most of its initial condition inside a hundred ticks. 6. The camera is not the world

Last idea before the code. The simulation buffer and the image on your screen are two different things: the buffer is the world, the Image pass is a camera pointed at it. The camera can show the world one-square-per-pixel (the naive thing), or it can zoom: draw each world-square as a bunch of screen pixels. Your Day 449 does exactly that — its camera reads the buffer at 15% of the screen coordinate, blowing every square up into a fat ~7-pixel block. That's why your automaton looks so pleasingly chunky, and it's the same trick the microscope demo above uses (there, I also shrink the world itself so the GPU doesn't bother simulating squares you'll never see — a difference worth knowing: your version simulates the whole full-resolution world but only photographs its bottom-left corner).

7. The code, at last

Now — and only now — the code, which at this point should read like a summary of the page. Two passes. Buffer A is the world (sections 1–5); Image is the camera (section 6). Setup: Buffer A's iChannel0 = Buffer A itself.

// ---- Buffer A: the world ------------------------------------------

// the hash: chaotic arithmetic pretending to be dice (section 5)
#define rnd(x) fract(1e3*sin((x + iTime*0.1)*mat2(2,13.51,73.37,-57.17)))

// "consult the photograph": was the square at p on, last tick? (section 3)
int cell(ivec2 p) {
    ivec2 R = ivec2(iResolution.xy);
    p = (p + R) % R;              // donut world — walk off one edge, return on the other
    return texelFetch(iChannel0, p, 0).x > 0.5 ? 1 : 0;
}

void mainImage(out vec4 O, vec2 U)
{
    ivec2 px = ivec2(U);          // the square I am standing on (section 3)

    // tick zero: one click of the dice, 50% density (section 5)
    if (iFrame == 0) { O = vec4(rnd(U).x < 0.5 ? 1. : 0.); return; }

    // question one: count the diamond — eight addresses,
    // eight lines, laid out like the map in section 4
    int k =
      cell(px+ivec2( 0,-2)) + cell(px+ivec2(-1,-1)) + cell(px+ivec2( 1,-1))
    + cell(px+ivec2(-2, 0))                         + cell(px+ivec2( 2, 0))
    + cell(px+ivec2(-1, 1)) + cell(px+ivec2( 1, 1)) + cell(px+ivec2( 0, 2));

    // question two: am I on?
    bool on = texelFetch(iChannel0, px, 0).x > 0.5;

    // question three: Conway's law (section 2)
    O = vec4(k == 3 || (on && k == 2) ? 1. : 0.);
}
// ---- Image: the camera (section 6) --------------------------------
void mainImage(out vec4 O, vec2 U)
{
    ivec2 p = ivec2(U * 0.15);    // zoom: every world-square becomes ~7 screen pixels
    O = vec4(texelFetch(iChannel0, p, 0).rgb, 1);
}

This is your Day 449 with three honest annotations, because the differences between it and the listing above are worth understanding rather than just quietly absorbing:

The wrap line. Your version computes R in cell() but never actually uses it — there's no (p + R) % R. Reading outside a texture with texelFetch is undefined behaviour in GLSL; on most hardware it hands back zero, which makes your world's edges behave like walls of permanently dead squares. That's a legitimate universe! (Patterns die when they hit the border instead of wrapping back in.) But it's an accident, not a decision, and it can differ from GPU to GPU. The one line above turns the world into a donut, which is the usual choice; delete it if you genuinely want walls — but then delete the unused R too, so the code says what it means.

The sampler dropdowns do nothing. Your Buffer A input is set to mipmap / repeat in the channel settings. texelFetch bypasses samplers entirely — no filtering, no wrap mode, ever. Those dropdowns only touch texture() calls. Harmless, but worth knowing that changing them will never change your output; wrap behaviour has to happen in code, like above.

The camera crops. U * 0.15 means the screen shows only the bottom-left 15% of the world while the GPU faithfully simulates the other 85% off-camera. Fine at this scale; if you ever zoom in harder, think about simulating a smaller world instead and zooming that (the microscope-demo approach) — same look, a fraction of the work. Centring the view is one more idea: ivec2(U * 0.15) + ivec2(iResolution.xy * 0.42) photographs the middle of the world instead of the corner.
